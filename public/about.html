Chapter the first

The short version

I think about forecasting for a living, I've previously written some science fiction, and I have a history of getting sucked into all kinds of different hobbies.

I'm Director of Forecasting at Metaculus, where I wound up after becoming obsessed with forecasting and doing pretty good at it. Before that I worked as a bridge engineer for six years, and got a master's degree in civil engineering from Iowa State University.

I love sci-fi and fantasy, and wrote my own sci-fi novel, SEER, when I had an idea I was really excited about and wanted to try my hand at writing a book. I live in Iowa with my wife and three kids.

Chapter the second

Before this

For about a decade I designed and inspected bridges across Iowa.

It is a profession that makes you honest. A load path either exists or it doesn't. A tolerance is not a matter of opinion. You learn to ask what you would have to believe for your own analysis to be wrong, and then you go and check, because the alternative is unacceptable.

I think about that habit constantly in work that has far softer feedback.

Chapter the third

Now

These days I coordinate forecasting programs at Metaculus.

Most of the job is not prediction. It is writing questions that can actually resolve, arguing about wording until the ambiguity is gone, and then finding out in public whether the crowd was right. It turns out that "what exactly would count as this happening?" is one of the more useful questions you can ask about almost anything.

Calibration is a habit, not a talent.

Chapter the fourth

The fiction

SEER began as a question I couldn't put down.

If a system could reliably predict what you wanted — not guess, not nudge, but know — would you still call it wanting? In the 22nd century the algorithm does exactly that, and almost nobody minds. The novel follows Maria, who is kidnapped by the people trying to bring it down and then has to work out which of them is telling her the truth.

Not Human is a shorter, colder cousin of the same question: Lisa is the first person in the world to have her mind copied to a machine, and the copy insists it is her.

Both are on the fiction page.
